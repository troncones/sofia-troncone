The deal

A $81.3B IPO priced at $83, almost entirely new shares (97.6%). With a concurrent $26.0B private placement, net proceeds come to about $104.8B, mostly for compute. It opened at $111.50, leaving about $27.9B on the table.

The business

Revenue went from $0 to $17.0B a quarter in about 3½ years. The gross margin is 85.5%, but R&D pushes the company to a small −3.8% operating margin. Each model helps build the next, a loop Anthropic calls its core advantage.

The catch

About $900.9B in compute commitments, chips concentrated at TSMC in Taiwan, the top 5 customers at about 25% of revenue, and a structure where public shareholders can't outvote the founders and the Trust.

Who benefited from the pricing?

$27.93B

"Money left on the table." The opening price was $28.50 above the IPO price, multiplied by 980M base shares. That is roughly what investors who got allocations at $83 gained at the first trade, and what Anthropic and the selling holders gave up by not pricing higher. I was on the winning side of this: I got an IPO allocation at $83, so my share gained $28.50 (+34.3%) at the first trade.

Why go public?

I believe Anthropic went public because they want to gain trust and raise cash flow.

Raise cash
  • 97.6% of the base deal is new shares, so $79.38B goes to Anthropic itself.
  • The prospectus puts net proceeds from the IPO plus the Class N placement at about $104.8B, or $117.0B if the underwriters buy all of their extra shares.
  • The stated uses are working capital, compute and infrastructure, hiring and keeping talent, possible acquisitions, and $4.3B in employee stock tax withholding.
  • Only 2.4% goes to insiders, so this IPO is about funding the company, not cashing out.
Gain trust
  • A public company must publish audited financials and disclose its risks to the SEC, so customers and investors can check the numbers.
  • It faces ongoing quarterly reporting and public scrutiny.
  • Its public-benefit structure is now on the record in the prospectus.
  • Anthropic's own stated purposes include to "create a public market" for its stock and "enable access to the public equity markets."

Sources: Pricing FWP · "Use of Proceeds," final prospectus, p. 129.

Reading the prospectus

The investment case compared with the financial evidence and the disclosed risks.

Prospectus summary: my reading vs Claude's

Claude's summary: 6 main points
  1. What it is. Anthropic is a research lab first. Its mission is the "responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." Its product is Claude, sold through models, a developer platform and apps.
  2. Hypergrowth. Revenue went from $0 in Q1 2023 to $17.0B in Q3 2026 alone. It grew 1,127% year over year, and net revenue retention is about 900%.
  3. Enterprise focus. More than 11,900 customers spend $100K+ a year, more than 1,900 spend $1M+, and about 250 spend $10M+. Claude Code and Claude Cowork are its "Virtual Collaborators."
  4. Huge compute bet. About $900.9B is committed for compute and infrastructure, with $70.7B due within 12 months. It already had $95.6B in cash before the IPO.
  5. Self-reinforcing loops. Customer feedback improves the models, revenue pays for compute, and each Claude model helps build the next ("closing the loop").
  6. Unusual governance. It is a public benefit corporation. A Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board, and the founders hold 50.1% of the vote through one special share. Public Class A shareholders have very little control.
Your take: where does your own reading agree or disagree with these points? What did you notice that the summary missed?

Source: Prospectus Summary, final prospectus, pp. 7–19.

Strongest claim in the investment case

Candidates from the summary
  • Growth: $0 to $17.0B in quarterly revenue in about 3½ years, described as the "fastest enterprise adoption of any technology in history."
  • Compute efficiency: revenue grew 1,127% while compute costs grew 382%, so each dollar of compute is earning more.
  • Customer expansion: about 900% net revenue retention, meaning existing customers spend about 9× what they did a year earlier.
  • Operating efficiency: $6.6M of revenue per employee, up from $1.4M a year earlier.
My take

I chose compute efficiency: revenue grew 1,127% while compute costs grew 382%. But I actually think compute costs will overpower revenue. Maybe it will become a break-even.

Model releases and revenue growth

I think the models will keep getting better and then eventually flatline for a bit. I see this as a positive feedback loop.

The loop, in the prospectus
  • Improvement loop: customers use Claude, their feedback improves the models, and better models win more customers.
  • Investment loop: more revenue pays for more compute and research, which leads to better models and more revenue.
  • "Closing the loop": each Claude model helps build the next one.
Evidence it's working
  • Revenue went from $0 at launch (Q1 2023) to $17.0B in a single quarter (Q3 2026).
  • Revenue grew 1,127% year over year, while compute costs grew 382%.
  • Opus 5.5 cut customer cost per task by about 40% compared with Opus 5. Lower cost brings more usage.

Source: Prospectus Summary, final prospectus, pp. 7–13 ("Our Self-Reinforcing Loops," "Strengths of Our Financial Model").

Before R&D: yes
  • In the first nine months of 2026, the gross margin was 85.5%. Serving customers costs about 14¢ per dollar of revenue.
  • Revenue minus cost of revenue, sales & marketing and G&A (everything except R&D) leaves $17.98B, a 54.1% margin.
After R&D: not yet
  • R&D of $19.24B wipes that out, leaving an operating loss of $1.26B (−3.8%).
  • That's a big improvement on the −205% operating margin a year earlier.

Can R&D be separated from the product?

Anthropic says no. Customers stay "based on whether our models are at the frontier," so "ongoing model development [is] a critical factor in revenue generation." Model development frees compute for serving customers, and customer use feeds back into research. If you stop R&D, the product falls behind and the 85.5% gross margin probably won't last. So the honest answer is operating profit, which is still slightly negative, not the gross margin.

What's new compared with a 1997 internet IPO: Amazon's risks were about a young business: losses, competition, managing growth. Anthropic discloses those too, but it also lists risks no 1997 company could have had. Its own product might act on its own. It must find hundreds of billions of dollars of compute. And outside shareholders effectively can't change who controls the company.

My take: the risk that stood out

TSMC and Taiwan. What happens if there is an attack on the locations where the chips are made, a natural disaster, a cyber attack, war, etc.?

What the prospectus says
  • "The majority of the world's most advanced chip manufacturing capacity is concentrated in Taiwan, primarily at Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company."
  • Risks named in the filing: "earthquakes and typhoons," "geopolitical tensions between Taiwan and China," and interruptions to Taiwan's "power grid, water supply, or other critical infrastructure."
  • "Any military conflict… blockade, natural disaster, or other incident affecting Taiwan could severely disrupt the global supply of advanced chips, with effects that could persist for years."
  • Building chip plants outside Taiwan "remain[s] in early stages."
  • It has already happened on a small scale: Anthropic has "not always been able to obtain sufficient quantities of chips on a timely basis."

Source: Risk Factors, final prospectus. Taiwan is mentioned 21 times in the filing.

Control and the bet

Who controls Anthropic, and the assumptions behind its growth.

Who controls Anthropic?

Board of directorsMakes the company's decisions · majority independentMust balance shareholders, people affected, and the mission
You: Class A holders1 vote per shareOutvoted on general matters by design
  • The board runs the company. As a PBC, it must balance shareholders' financial interests, the people affected by Anthropic's conduct, and the mission.
  • The LTBT picks directors but has no role in day-to-day management or strategy. It is meant to pick directors who will strike that balance well.
  • The founders control 50.1% of the vote on general matters through one Class F share. Together with the LTBT, they can elect the entire board.
  • Anthropic is a Nasdaq "controlled company," so it can skip some governance rules. Its nominating and governance committee won't be fully independent.

Source: "Our Governance Structure and Its Goals," pp. 14–16, final prospectus.

Owning vs. controlling: Class A and N hold essentially all the economic value (dividends, sale proceeds). Class T and F hold essentially all the control but almost none of the money. They're worth only par value. The usual link between owning a share and having a say is deliberately cut.

Why would Amazon and Google accept non-voting shares?

  • They're also partners. Anthropic runs on AWS Trainium and Google TPUs, and Google has a commercial agreement with Anthropic. Their investments help secure business for their cloud and chip divisions.
  • The economic upside is real. Google's notes converted into non-voting Series E-3 and E-4 preferred stock at $44.87 and $50.48 a share, compared with today's $83 IPO price.
  • It may help with regulators (my inference, not in the filing). A big tech company holding a voting stake in a rival AI lab could raise antitrust concerns. Non-voting shares may reduce that risk.
My take: owning without control

Yes, I'm okay owning shares I can't vote with. I feel a sense of protection with this.

Sources: "Our Capital Structure," p. 17; Note 9 (Google convertible notes), p. F-41, final prospectus. Class E is left out for simplicity.

Demonstrated

  • Recursive self-improvement keeps speeding up rather than stalling.
  • "A country of geniuses in a data center."
  • Demand grows fast enough to cover $900.9B of compute commitments through 2045.
